If there's a perfect moment to talk about working with IT in the public sector, it's now. 2024-2025 has officially entered the history books as **the Golden Age of technology public exams**.
That's not an exaggeration; it's statistics, context and historical necessity.

Brazil is going through something similar to what the US experienced in the 2000s with the digital transformation of the State:
the government realized late, but it did realize, that without technology, there's no functioning country.

Hospitals depend on systems, courts depend on systems, public policies depend on systems.
And when everything depends on systems… guess what?
There aren't enough people. Not nearly enough.

Let's look at the complete picture, but with the honesty that prep courses don't have the courage to put in their PDFs.

---

## 1. What Made This "Golden Age" Possible?

For decades, the Brazilian government lived comfortably on top of legacy:

* Delphi systems running on a dusty server,
* lost scripts nobody knows who wrote in 2004,
* databases with no documentation,
* integrations held together with "hot glue and faith".

But then came:

* hacker attacks on STJ, the Health Ministry, DNIT, Datasus,
* the explosion of data,
* the pressure for digital government,
* the popularization of the cloud,
* and the social demand for efficiency.

The result?

**An historic demand for technology professionals in the public sector.**

The CNU (National Unified Exam) alone opened hundreds of positions.
Add SERPRO, Dataprev, BB, Caixa, Ministries, Courts, TRFs… it became a tsunami.

---

## 2. Salaries: the Truth Behind the Numbers (RJU included)

Salary has become a sensitive, murky topic.
So let's make it crystal clear, with context and no fanfiction.

### Technician / Support Level: R$ 4,000 – R$ 6,000

Usually in smaller federal agencies or city halls. It's the entry point.

### Analyst (Federal Executive, Ministries, State-owned like Dataprev and SERPRO): R$ 9,000 – R$ 16,000

This is where most IT civil servants enter.

**RJU (Single Legal Regime)**, for federal statutory servants:

* job stability
* differentiated retirement
* annual progression
* bonuses (qualification, hazardous work, etc.)

In state-owned companies (like SERPRO), it's **CLT** (private-sector regime), but with profit sharing that boosts your take-home pay.

### The Elite: Oversight, Inspection and Legislative Power (R$ 20,000 – R$ 30,000+)

Courts of Audit, Tax Inspectors, Revenue Service, Senate.

In this range, IT becomes a tool for decision power:
data analytics to find fraud, algorithm auditing, forensic expertise.

---

## 3. Okay, but What's the Work Like? There Are 3 "Brazils" Inside Public IT

And whoever doesn't understand this usually ends up frustrated. Let's look at each one:

---

### A. The Management Brazil: The "Contract Inspector"

Here you don't program.
And you need to understand that without romanticizing it.

Your role is:

* writing requirements,
* analyzing proposals,
* overseeing outsourced companies,
* having meetings,
* sending documents for validation,
* making sure nothing illegal happens in IT contracting.

It's almost a fusion of **Product Owner + Requirements Analyst + Public Inspector**.

A lot of people who dreamed of an IDE end up disappointed.

---

### B. The Code Brazil: The "Hands-On"

SERPRO, Dataprev, BNDES, BB, Caixa, Prodam.

Here you:

* open the IDE,
* make commits,
* spin up containers,
* configure pipelines,
* deal with architecture,
* solve critical bugs.

It's the "public sector that looks like the private sector".

Less politics.
More code.
And more profit sharing.

---

### C. The Audit Brazil: The Salary Olympus

TCU, CGU, Revenue Service, Federal Police (forensics).

Here the work is highly technical and investigative:

* rebuilding systems to find fraud,
* auditing AI algorithms,
* analyzing billions of records,
* tracking financial operations.

It's CSI, IT version, with a smiling paycheck.

## 4. What Are They Asking For? (And Why Exactly That?)

2025 is bringing hiring announcements focused on:

### Data and AI (the new oil of the Union)

* Python
* Machine Learning
* Power BI
* Data Lake
* Spark

The government realized it has **the largest databases in Latin America** and doesn't know how to use them.

### Development

* Java/Spring (the cathedral of legacy)
* C#/.NET
* Python/Django
* JS (React/Angular)

### Security

Exploded after the cyber attacks.
The country ran to catch up.

### Infra + Cloud

Docker, Kubernetes, AWS, Azure.

Yes: we're leaving the **"server in the basement"** era.

## 5. The Naked and Cruel Reality

Nobody tells you this, but they should.

### The bad side

* You may land in an agency using Delphi + Access database
* You may become an "Analyst who doesn't analyze" (just clicks "accept" on tasks in SEI)
* You may get a politically appointed boss
* You may be stuck at the constitutional cap (no infinite private-sector salary explosions)

### The good side

* **Real stability**
* **Massive remote work**
* **Low workload**
* **Zero after-hours pressure**
* **The possibility of living anywhere while earning a capital-city salary**

## 6. The Needed Education: Top University or "Just the Diploma" University?

This is a debate that has simmered for years, and it makes sense to fit it here, because public IT lives off both worlds at the same time.

### Let's start with the historical truth

For a long time, the public sector valued **the diploma for the diploma's sake**.
"Got a bachelor's? Great. You're in."
Technical knowledge came later.

The private sector, on the other hand, moved toward the **"skills first"** model: if you know how to do it, you work. A diploma is an accessory.

Today both models coexist, and that's where the confusion is born.

---

### Argument 1: Going to a good, hard, deep university (CS, Engineering, strong IS)

#### Advantages of strong education

* Gives you a theoretical foundation that 95% of the market doesn't have
* Forms a professional who understands **why** things work, not just *how to do them*
* Creates absurd technical autonomy: you don't get lost when the trendy technology dies
* Prepares you for heavier exams (like TCU, Revenue Service, Senate)
* Builds extremely high-level networking
* Sets you apart for strategic positions or applied research

#### Disadvantages of strong education

* It's hard, it drains you psychologically
* It takes time
* It doesn't always teach what the market asks for in the short term
* It can be frustrating if you want immediate results

---

### Argument 2: Attending any college just to meet the requirement and studying tech on your own

#### Advantages of the practical path

* Much faster to enter the market
* You focus 100% on the skills that land jobs
* It's the perfect path for those who want to go to companies, startups and banks
* For public exams, having the diploma is enough; nobody asks which university

#### Disadvantages of the practical path

* Lack of foundation can limit your long-term growth
* You can become a professional who "solves known problems", but freezes on new ones
* It may not prepare you for deeper careers (hardcore data science, advanced security, technical auditing)

---

### But in the end, which is better?

**The honest answer, and my opinion:**

It depends on the type of professional you want to become and how you want to work.

If you want:

* deep technical autonomy,
* to understand the why of things,
* to solve complex problems,
* to work in top agencies (TCU, Federal Police, Revenue Service),
* or to climb the career ladder until you're a reference…

**A strong university matters.
It makes a difference.
And it stays with you for decades.**

Now, if you want:

* to enter the market fast,
* to work with current technology,
* to build a portfolio,
* to focus directly on employability,
* to pass mid-level or analyst exams…

**Any college works.
What matters is your practice and your skills.**

In the end: **there's no universal path. There's the path that fits the professional you want to be.**

## 7. The Future of the Public Sector (Spoiler: It's About Data and AI)

The government is swimming in data and doesn't know how to swim.

TCU, CGU, Revenue Service and Courts are changing their positions to:

* Algorithm Auditor
* Intelligent Systems Inspector
* Data Analytics Analyst
* AI Governance Specialist

The State will need people who understand the code **and** understand the social/legal impact of that code.
