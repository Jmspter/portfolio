## What Made Me Investigate Databricks

I was scrolling through Twitter, that natural habitat where developers mix venting with epiphanies, when I stumbled on a comment that caught my attention. The person said something like:

> "First impressions with Databricks:
> I'm enchanted
> you can do everything there: modeling, pipelines, versioning
> my first contact with Spark was magical and now I want to use it for absolutely everything
> the free edition limits me too much in running jobs and clusters and that makes me sad."

That's when a spark of technical curiosity hit me: *okay, if someone is describing Spark as "magical", something interesting is going on*. Because let's be honest, Spark was never exactly famous for its "magic". It's powerful, sure; elegant, sometimes; but magical? Only when everything works on the first try, which is almost never.

So I went investigating. And the more I read, the more I realized Databricks has become, in recent years, one of those tools that triggers a curious phenomenon: **it's half hype, half serious technical foundation**, and precisely for that reason it deserves an honest, contextualized and even somewhat skeptical look.

After all, a lot of people talk about Databricks as if it were a mix of AWS and Hogwarts, but few people sit down to explain *why* it became the favorite platform of data engineers, ML teams and companies that want to do serious things with Big Data without suffering (or suffering a little less).

And of course, when something starts becoming a trend, my head does what it always does: gathers practical examples, compares it with the reality of the market, remembers stories nobody tells and tries to separate solid engineering from technological romanticizing.

## The Lakehouse Concept: The Truth Nobody Tells

To start the right way, you need to understand the concept that transformed the market: the **Lakehouse**.

Before Databricks appeared, the industry lived a somewhat childish war between Data Warehouse and Data Lake:

- **Data Warehouse**: organized, expensive, full of rules
- **Data Lake**: cheap, flexible and as messy as my bedroom

Databricks looked at the scenario and said: why not combine the best of both? The result became the industry standard. Today everyone tries to copy it, including giants that used to turn their noses up at it.

And that's no coincidence. The creators of Databricks are the same people behind Apache Spark, which had already changed the game once. The platform is basically the premium, polished, supercharged version of Spark, rewritten and optimized for the real world.

## The Architecture: Control Plane vs Data Plane

The second piece that sets Databricks apart is the architecture. Who hasn't asked themselves why on earth there's a Control Plane and a Data Plane? They sound like names invented by someone trying to impress in an interview. But this is what separates adults from amateurs.

- **Control Plane**: stays with Databricks. It's the interface, the settings, the pretty stuff.
- **Data Plane**: lives in your cloud. Your data never leaves your account.

This changes the game because, at the end of the day, your security team sleeps better. And developers like sleeping better.

## The Brazilian Market and Mass Adoption

Now, if we look at the Brazilian market, the story gets even more interesting. Because Brazil adopted Databricks the same way it adopted WhatsApp: it went from nowhere to everywhere.

**Companies using Databricks in Brazil:**

- Itaú
- Nubank
- iFood
- Globo
- Magalu

If there's data, someone plugged Databricks into it. And not just out of trend, but out of necessity. The amount of data generated by these players is absurd. If you try to process that with a traditional approach, it becomes a catastrophe.

That's why there's a race for professionals who understand Spark, Delta Lake, Photon and the rest of the alphabet soup. And that's why salaries skyrocketed.

## The Cold War: Databricks vs Snowflake

Abroad, the story is different, but equally curious. Databricks and Snowflake live in a cold war:

- **Snowflake**: started as the darling of SQL analysts
- **Databricks**: dominated engineers and scientists

Now both try to invade each other's territory. Except Databricks noticed a turning point before most: the generative AI era. So it bought MosaicML for over a billion dollars to make training large models cheaper. While some think about colorful dashboards, Databricks thinks about training your company's next LLM.

## The Hidden Universe Behind the Interface

A lot of people use Databricks as if it were a Jupyter Notebook on steroids and think they're already advanced. But there's a hidden universe behind the interface that separates seniors from beginners:

- **Asset Bundles**: the magic of declaring an entire infrastructure as code. If you're still clicking around to create a job, you're playing around.
- **Delta Live Tables**: turns brittle pipelines into declarative, resilient flows.
- **Z-Ordering and Liquid Clustering**: physical optimizations that solve pain nobody notices until the storage and processing bill explodes.
- **Time Travel**: saves careers when someone deletes a table by accident.
- **AI functions right in SQL**: make any analyst look like a wizard.

## The Power of Photon

And then comes Photon. This is where Databricks shows its muscle.

Spark was born on the JVM. Fantastic, elegant, robust. Just limited. Databricks rewrote the entire engine in C++ and named it Photon.

**Result:**

- Queries that run several times faster
- Cost reduction because the cluster lives less time
- Less time turned on = less money burned

## The Story Behind Databricks

This whole story isn't just technical. It has a behind-the-scenes:

1. The project was born in Berkeley
2. It became open source
3. It grew in an academic lab
4. It moved to a billion-dollar startup
5. Today it dictates standards like Delta Lake

Databricks even open-sourced Delta Lake to avoid claims they were locking customers in.

## Strengths and Weaknesses

### Strengths

- Fast, flexible, scalable
- Brings everyone together on a single platform
- Makes governance easier
- Creates a more mature data culture

### Weaknesses

- **Treacherous cost**: one forgotten cluster over a weekend and the bill arrives the size of a Nubank debt.
- **Steep learning curve** for people who only know SQL
- **Clusters taking minutes to start** annoy anyone who's used BigQuery or Snowflake (though serverless is improving this)

## How to Start

To get started, the path is simple, but not easy:

1. Use the Community Edition
2. Really learn PySpark
3. Understand Delta Lake
4. Explore DABs and DLT before you think you're ready
5. Get certified not because the market demands it, but because it will force you to learn in a structured, consistent way

If you've read my other articles, you already know my goal here isn't to sell promises. It's to show what happens in this senseless madness that is the tech industry.

And Databricks deserves honesty. It's not just another tool. It's a central piece of the future of data. But don't expect glamour, don't expect shortcuts and don't expect it to solve everything alone. At the end of the day, Databricks is just a powerful hammer. Understanding how to use it is what separates the person who hits the nail from the person who builds the whole house.

## Conclusion

At the end of the day, working with data, code and infrastructure always comes back to the same question: are you using the right tool for the right problem? Because if you're not, the bill arrives. And it arrives fast. I've seen people build a simple CRUD with Kafka, Spark and three async queues just because it looked professional. The result was exactly what you'd expect: a heavy, expensive, slow project that's impossible to maintain, all to solve something that a handful of routes in .NET or Rails would deliver with ease. On the other side, I've also seen the opposite. Huge systems, full of events, high traffic, multiple integrations, all running on something way too small. A PHP monolith solving things that required parallelism and distributed processing. It worked for a while, until traffic doubled. Then came bursting queues, timeouts, deployments that took down the entire system. Scalability isn't about being fancy, it's about not imploding when the number of users changes.

And this is where the part nobody talks about comes in. There's no point in building an architectural army when your team has three people. There's no point splitting the product into ten micro-SaaS if you don't have people to maintain ten repositories, ten pipelines, ten deploys and ten sources of bugs. That becomes debt, it becomes cost, it becomes burnout. I already wrote about this in the [microservices](https://jamespeter.dev/blog/microservices-architecture) article. The summary is simple. Use the tool that solves a problem the size the problem actually is. If the solution looks too sophisticated for something simple, you're digging your own grave. If it looks too simple for something complex, you're asking to suffer a few months from now. Technical maturity isn't about choosing the most famous technology. It's about choosing the most appropriate one.
