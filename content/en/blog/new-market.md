It’s been a while, but we’re back.

A few months ago, I wrote about the tech market and about how working in IT has always been, in a way, mining in the dark.

We enter a mine without really knowing where the gold is. We learn one technology, then another, switch frameworks, change companies, change specializations. When we finally start understanding how things work, someone announces that a technology is “dead” and that now we need to learn another one.

And we keep digging.

In that text, my conclusion was simple: stability has never really been the strong point of technology.

Now I want to continue that conversation, because things have changed since then. And perhaps the mine has become even bigger.

## The first thing that changed was the question

Recently, someone asked me:

> “What do I need to learn to work in IT?”

Before answering, I stopped to think about the current state of the market. And for the first time in a long while, I couldn’t quickly come up with an answer.

I started asking myself:

What would I do today if I were starting from zero?

The question sounds simple, but it isn’t.

With the arrival of artificial intelligence, the value of a significant part of the work that used to justify certain roles has changed. Boilerplate became cheap. Documentation became cheap. Tests became cheaper. Repetitive code became cheap. Prototyping became cheap. Even learning a particular technology became cheaper.

And when something becomes cheap, an unavoidable question appears:

What remains expensive?

Perhaps this is one of the most important questions for anyone working in technology today.

In the end, I answered that the ideal would be to do some market research before trying to answer.

And that’s exactly what I started doing.

The result ended up becoming the basis for this article.

## AI didn’t kill IT. It changed where value is

There’s a certain obsession with asking whether artificial intelligence will replace programmers.

I think that question is too small.

The more interesting question is:

What part of a programmer’s work is no longer valuable?

Because programming has never been just writing code.

Code is a tool. Just as a calculator didn’t replace mathematicians, a compiler didn’t replace engineers, and a spreadsheet didn’t turn everyone into accountants.

Tools change the cost of doing certain things. And when the cost changes, the entire market starts reorganizing itself.

If, before, a company needed five people to build something, perhaps now it can do it with three. That doesn’t necessarily mean two people became useless. It means the cost of producing software changed.

And maybe this is exactly where one of the biggest transformations we’re starting to see lies.

The discussion stops being only about who can write code faster and becomes about who can better understand the problem, make better decisions, and build systems that actually make sense.

## The curious story of software becoming more expensive to maintain

There’s an interesting irony in the history of computing.

We spent decades creating tools to make development simpler. We created better languages, frameworks, libraries, cloud infrastructure, containers, CI/CD, DevOps, serverless, and a huge number of abstractions to reduce the effort required to put software into the world.

And it worked.

Perhaps even too well.

Today we can put a system into production in a few hours. The problem is that we can also create an extremely complex system in a few hours.

It’s like inventing a machine that automatically builds houses and then discovering a few years later that there are now millions of houses and we need an entire system to manage water, electricity, traffic, and maintenance.

Complexity didn’t disappear.

It just moved.

And maybe artificial intelligence will accelerate this process even further. If creating software became much cheaper, we may quickly reach a scenario where the problem is no longer a lack of systems, but an excess of them.

We may end up with thousands of small services, agents, automations, and integrations running at the same time, all seemingly useful, but creating a layer of complexity that someone will still need to understand and manage.

## And this is where opportunities start to appear

If writing code is becoming cheaper, other parts of the work begin to gain importance.

It’s no longer enough to be able to produce an API in seconds. Someone must decide whether that API should exist, where the data will live, how much it will cost, who will have access, and what happens when part of the system fails.

It’s also necessary to understand what happens when the internet goes down, when the database becomes unavailable, or when an AI model responds with something completely wrong.

And eventually, someone will get that classic call:

> “Why did the bill come in ten times higher this month?”

The machine can write the code.

We still need someone to look after the child.

That’s why architecture, infrastructure, security, data, observability, performance, integration, and business understanding may become even more important as code production becomes cheaper.

At the core, there’s an important difference between knowing how to build something and knowing whether something should even be built.

Artificial intelligence can help a lot with the first question.

The second remains very human.

## Artificial intelligence needs a home

Perhaps this is one of the opportunities that are going unnoticed in the middle of all the AI noise.

Everyone wants to talk about the model.

Very few people want to talk about where the model will live.

But artificial intelligence needs infrastructure. It needs servers, GPUs, memory, storage, networks, data centers, energy, cooling, observability, security, and orchestration.

And money.

A lot of money.

AI can seem like a purely digital thing when you interact with it in a browser. But there is a considerable amount of physical infrastructure behind that response.

In the end, even artificial intelligence has to pay the electricity bill.

This opens space for professionals in infrastructure, cloud, SRE, security, networking, HPC, performance engineering, and data. And perhaps this is one of the most interesting characteristics of this new phase: some of the most important opportunities may be in the things the end user never sees.

The next revolution in computing may be sold as software.

But someone still has to build the socket.

## Maybe the future is less “IT” than it seems

There’s another change I consider even more interesting.

For a long time, it seemed like a career in technology had to happen inside a tech company. You’d work at a startup, a software house, a big tech company, or a SaaS company.

But technology is entering almost everything.

Energy, industry, agriculture, logistics, health, finance, engineering, automation, and scientific research are increasingly dependent on software and infrastructure.

Perhaps one of the biggest opportunities in the coming years lies exactly in the combination between technology and something you deeply understand.

Technology + energy is different from just technology.

Technology + engineering is different.

Technology + law is different.

Because the more technology becomes a common tool, the more important it becomes to deeply understand the problem we’re trying to solve with it.

And this makes me think about an area I particularly believe will still produce a good conversation: AI and embedded systems programming.

We’re used to imagining artificial intelligence running on large servers, data centers full of GPUs, and massive cloud structures. But there’s another path happening in parallel: putting more and more intelligence directly into devices.

Sensors, vehicles, industrial machines, medical equipment, energy systems, IoT devices, and an infinity of equipment that need to make decisions locally.

In this scenario, the problem is no longer just “how do we write the software?”

It also involves memory constraints, processing, power consumption, communication, response time, reliability, and even what happens when the device is completely disconnected from the internet. Literally, it’s like programming with assembly in the 1950s while the big rollback is in action.

It’s a huge topic to discuss here without turning this article into something else.

So maybe we’ll save that for next time.

AI and embedded systems.

The mine still has tunnels we haven’t even started exploring.

## Maybe the next competitive advantage is doing less

There’s a curious contradiction happening.

We have tools capable of producing software faster than ever, and at the same time, we’re increasingly concerned with complexity.

Perhaps the next generation of good professionals won’t be the one who knows fifty tools. Maybe it will be the one who knows when not to use forty-nine of them.

I’m not interested in defending a technology just because it won a fictional war against another.

The best language is the one that pays my salary.

At the end of the day, a programming language is a tool.

What interests me are technologies that allow us to solve problems without turning every problem into a small infrastructure company.

Sometimes a simple solution is really just a simple solution.

And that seems trivial.

Until you spend three months maintaining the complicated one.

## The new IT professional may be a hybrid

For a long time, specialization was treated like a kind of destiny.

Backend, frontend, database, infrastructure, security, data.

I increasingly believe the boundaries between these things will continue to become less rigid.

This doesn’t mean everyone needs to know everything.

Maybe that would be nice.

But it’s not impossible.

It’s just unlikely.

But the professional who understands a little of software, infrastructure, security, business, and automation starts to see the whole system.

And that matters.

Because when a tool becomes smarter, it doesn’t necessarily eliminate the professional who understands the problem. It can eliminate the professional who only knew how to execute a small mechanical part of it.

That difference is enormous.

## But there’s a problem that nobody likes to discuss

The entry point.

Going back to the question I was asked about what is needed to begin in the field today, I followed a line of reasoning that bothers me quite a bit.

If AI can write part of the code that used to be assigned to a junior developer, how will that junior developer gain the experience they need?

For years, beginners learned by doing repetitive tasks: creating CRUDs, writing tests, fixing small bugs, performing maintenance, and reading other people’s code.

These tasks seemed insignificant.

But they were exactly where a lot of people learned.

If part of that work is automated, we need to discover a new way to train professionals.

Because there is no expert without juniors.

And there is no specialist who appeared fully formed.

This may be one of the major issues of the next decade.

Not only:

> “Will AI replace people?”

But:

> “How do we train the people who will work alongside it?”

This question is especially important because there is a kind of paradox happening.

We want experienced professionals to work with artificial intelligence, but part of the experience those people acquired in the past came precisely from tasks that we are now trying to automate.

Perhaps the model of training needs to change together with the market.

## So where are the opportunities?

I don’t think a definitive list exists.

And I’m quite suspicious of any list that says which will be the “ten professions of the future.” Technology has an impressive talent for destroying predictions.

Still, some directions seem quite clear.

There is room in infrastructure for AI, cloud, HPC, performance engineering, cybersecurity, data, automation, distributed systems, observability, AI engineering, AI security, and integration of AI into traditional companies.

But looking at all of this, there is something I consider even more interesting: professionals capable of combining technology with deep knowledge of some domain.

Not necessarily people who know everything.

But people who can cross boundaries.

There is also an important difference between talented people and people who build their careers mainly through discipline and persistence.

Each may have a different path.

Some people have extraordinary ease in particular areas and can reach impressive performance peaks. Others may not have the same initial ease, but they compensate with consistency, curiosity, and willingness to keep learning.

In practice, both paths can make a difference.

Talent needs to be used.

Effort needs to be directed.

And perhaps the most important question is figuring out where each person can create the most value.

In the end, it doesn’t help to have an extraordinary tool if no one knows where to use it.

## The next gold rush may be different

In the gold rush, a lot of people got rich.

But not necessarily the miners.

There were those who sold tools, built roads, transported goods, supplied food, manufactured equipment, and built infrastructure.

Perhaps history is repeating itself.

The next great opportunity of artificial intelligence does not need to be only in building models. It can be in everything we need to build around them.

And this is important because it means we don’t necessarily need to run after the same thing everyone else is running after.

You don’t need to build the next foundational model.

You can build the infrastructure that uses it, protect the system, optimize its execution, integrate the technology into an industry, or solve a problem no one has noticed yet.

You can even simply use the technology to do better what you already know how to do.

Perhaps this last option is one of the most interesting.

Not every opportunity needs to create a new profession.

Sometimes it simply makes an existing profession much more powerful.

## After a long time, we’re back

When I finished the previous article, I wrote that working in technology was like a kind of mining in the dark. I still think that comparison makes sense, but perhaps the difference now is that we have machines capable of helping us dig. This didn’t make the mine less dangerous; if anything, it may have increased the speed with which we can open new tunnels, find unexpected paths, and eventually discover we were digging in the wrong direction.

Some technologies will disappear, some professions will shrink, and others will emerge. Some companies will find genuinely productive ways to use artificial intelligence, while others will probably just put a chatbot on top of a product and call it digital transformation. At the same time, some technologies that seem old today will continue to work perfectly, as almost always happens in technology. Maybe Ruby will still be around. Maybe some language few people are looking at today will become important tomorrow.

I still don’t have answers to many of the questions about where all this is heading. And honestly, I think that’s one of the most interesting parts. After years working in technology, I concluded that trying to predict exactly where the gold is probably matters less than learning to recognize a new vein when it appears.

Technology will continue to change, the market will remain unstable, and artificial intelligence will likely transform things we can’t even imagine today. But there is something that remains regardless of the tool, the language, or the current trend: problems keep existing, and someone still needs to solve them.

Perhaps that is where a significant opportunity lies. Not necessarily in learning the next technology as soon as it appears, chasing the next trend, or trying to guess which language will win the next dispute. Maybe it’s more important to develop the ability to look at a problem, understand the context in which it exists, and ask, with a bit of humility: “Is there a better way to do this?”

At the end of the day, maybe that’s why some people keep digging. Not because they know exactly where the gold is, but because they like the process of discovering what’s on the other side of the stone. It’s a trait I consider very common in people who choose to work with technology: the curiosity to dismantle something just to understand how it works and then try to build a slightly better version of it.

After a long time, we’re back. The mine remains dark, but now we have better tools, faster machines, and a huge number of new paths to explore. That doesn’t mean we know where we’re headed. It just means we have more possibilities than before.

In the previous article, I ended by saying:

“Good luck to all of us who choose to keep mining in the dark.”

Today I think a little differently.

Luck helps, but maybe it isn’t enough.

If you’re just starting out, thinking about restarting, or even feeling left behind, perhaps the most important thing is to remember that technology has never been an area where anyone had to get everything right on the first try. Careers change, technologies disappear, companies close, projects fail, and sometimes what seems like a detour ends up leading to a much better path.

It is always possible to start over.

To the talented, use your talents. To the hardworking, keep working. And for those who can combine talent with discipline, maybe the challenge is learning to transform that combination into something that really makes a difference. Each person will find a different path, with phases of growth, periods of doubt, and some stones larger than expected.

In the end, perhaps that is what still makes sense in the mining metaphor: no one knows exactly where the gold is before they start digging.

The mine remains dark.

But we’re still here, with better tools, new questions, and many places we haven’t explored yet.

So let’s keep digging.

And who knows, at some point, we might find gold.
