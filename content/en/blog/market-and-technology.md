I've been hearing this with frightening frequency over the last few months. Close people, distant people, experienced devs, infrastructure professionals, IoT folks, data and security people. Good people. People who studied, kept up to date, delivered results. Leaving the tech field or, at the very least, seriously flirting with the idea.

The reason is almost always the same: instability.

And here's the first truth few people like to say out loud: **tech was never a stable field**. It only *seemed* stable during a few very specific periods in history.

Today we're living through another moment where the bill has come due.

---

## The false sense of stability

For a long time, mainly between 2010 and 2020, the narrative was created that technology was the "safe harbor". Professionals were scarce, salaries rose fast, every company needed devs, every startup seemed promising.

The pandemic intensified this artificially.
Companies hired as if infinite growth were guaranteed. Inflated teams, off-the-chart salaries, promises of "senior in six months". It looked like a gold rush.

But anyone who's read a bit of history knows: **every gold rush ends**.

This has happened before.

* The dot-com bubble bursting in the early 2000s
* The 2008 crisis, which hit corporate technology hard
* Now, post-pandemic, with high interest rates, less investment and companies returning to survival mode

The tech market is cyclical. It always has been.

The difference is that now it's **more fragmented than ever**.

---

## Before, firing a tech worker was expensive

There was a time when firing a tech professional was almost shooting yourself in the foot.

Every dev did everything their own way.
There was no standard.
Documentation was a luxury.
Clean code was the exception.

That's where the famous legacy code and historical hacks were born. Systems only one person understood. Programs nobody dared touch because "if it breaks, only João knows how to fix it".

That gave power to the professional.
And cost to the company.

Not today.

---

## Fragmentation changed everything

Today the field turned into a big corporate Lego.

You're no longer "the IT guy".
You're backend. Or frontend. Or DevOps. Or SRE. Or data engineer. Or security analyst.

Each piece isolated. Each role well defined. Each responsibility documented in tickets, pipelines, runbooks and playbooks.

From a technical point of view, this brought enormous gains.
From a human point of view, it brought a direct consequence:

**it became cheap to replace people**.

If a backend leaves, another backend comes in.
If a DevOps leaves, another DevOps comes in.
The product keeps running.

And now there's an important aggravating factor that can no longer be ignored: **Artificial Intelligence**.

With AI writing boilerplate, suggesting code, reviewing pull requests and speeding up repetitive tasks, companies' confidence in operating with smaller teams has grown dramatically. AI became the great accelerator of this commoditization of technical work. The "do more with less" narrative never had a stronger justification than now.

That doesn't mean AI replaces people.
But it means it **reduces the need for so many pieces on the board**.

A company has no heart.
It has SLA, margin and cost.

That turns the career into a real tightrope.

You can have more than 10 years of experience and still spend months unemployed.
Or land a new job, but with a salary below what you earned before.

And no, that doesn't mean you "became bad".
It means the market changed its mood.

Again.

---

## How people entered tech in each era (and why that matters)

Maybe the mistake is believing there was always a "right path" to building a career in technology. There wasn't. Each generation entered a different way, pushed by the circumstances of the time.

Way back in the 60s and 70s, the people who worked with computing didn't even call themselves "devs". They were mathematicians, physicists, engineers. Alan Turing didn't choose tech as a career; it simply didn't exist. He wanted to solve math problems and ended up creating the foundations of modern computing along the way.

In the 80s and 90s, a lot of people got in because they had a computer at home. They programmed in BASIC, Pascal and Assembly, not as a career but out of curiosity. They were self-taught out of necessity. Good courses were rare, documentation was physical books or magazines. Whoever persisted became a reference.

In the early 2000s, with the popularization of the internet, the "web developer" generation emerged. HTML, CSS, PHP, Java. Many people learned by building sites for local businesses, blogs, forums. The dot-com bubble burst, many left the field, others got hardened and carried on.

Between 2010 and 2020 came the era of frameworks, startups and the promise of infinite growth. Bootcamps, fast courses, accelerated careers. Many were drawn in by salary and demand. It worked for a while. Now we're seeing the adjustment.

None of those generations was wrong. They all just reacted to the historical context they lived in.

And that helps explain why so many people today are tired, frustrated or rethinking everything.

---

## The "Great Rollback"

Lately, a lot of people started talking about something called the *Great Rollback*. A sort of movement back to the essentials.

Fewer frameworks.
Less unnecessary complexity.
Smaller teams.
More demand for real productivity.

In theory, this could give power back to IT inside companies.

In practice, it can also mean something else:
**more responsibility concentrated in fewer people**.

Fewer people doing more work.
Less margin for error.
More silent pressure.

And, again, AI comes in as a constant backdrop. It doesn't create this movement, but it legitimizes it. If the machine helps write code, review logic and accelerate deliveries, the expectation on the human automatically rises. The problem isn't the tool, it's how the market uses it.

It's not exactly a paradise.

---

## When code becomes a commodity, what's left is the human

There's a detail lots of people ignore in this whole scenario.

When technique becomes Lego, what differentiates a professional is no longer just the code.

It's the ability to talk, negotiate, explain, align expectations, understand context and deal with people. Soft skills stop being a HR talking point and become a real survival mechanism. Those who usually resist "piece swapping" the most are the ones who know how to maneuver socially within companies.

Network, communication and reading the political environment of the workplace become as valuable as knowing how to program. Code got easier to replace. People who know how to deal with people? Not so much.

---

## Technology was always a strange cycle

Since Alan Turing, this field has worked like this.

Rapid advances.
Giant promises.
Deep crises.
Fresh starts.

It's a gold mine where the lights are always off.

You never know exactly if the next step will take you to a rich vein or a collapse.

And, being completely honest, you have to be a little crazy to choose to stay here.

If your only motivation is money, I'm sorry to say, but the chances of frustration are high.
Those who make good money and sleep well at night are, most of the time, entrepreneurs.
And even they have no guarantees.

---

## So why do I stay?

Because I like solving problems.

Simple as that.

And because, despite everything, new demands keep emerging.
New areas appear.
Others die out.

I've already said in another article that I'm focusing more on HPC and performance engineering. Not because it's easy. But because it's where I see depth, real difficulty and less hype.

That's not universal advice.
It's just the path that made sense to me.

---

## And if you want to leave, that's okay

This might be the most important part.

If you decide to leave the tech field, **you are not failing**.

What you learned here isn't lost.

I've seen people leave computing and become accountants, lawyers, teachers, managers. And all of them keep using, in some way, the logical reasoning, the analytical capacity and the problem-solving approach they learned in technology.

Tech doesn't trap you.
It transforms you.

---

## What I can say in the end

Stability isn't the strong point of the technology field.
At least not most of the time.

Those who stay, stay because they like the process.
Because they accept the uncertainty.
Because they understand this is, largely, a continuous bet.

I don't have a clear answer on how to prepare for everything that could happen.
If I said I did, I'd be lying.

It's mining in the dark.
It always was.

So, if you decide to keep digging, I can only say one thing:

**good luck to all of us who choose to keep mining in the dark.**
